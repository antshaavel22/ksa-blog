As you put on the VR headset or look at the massive floor-to-ceiling digital projections, you aren't just looking at art. You are walking directly inside the mind of Vincent van Gogh. It feels like absolute magic.

But as eye doctors and vision scientists, we have a little secret to share with you: the most incredible virtual reality headset in the world is already sitting right inside your head.

## How Your Eyes and Brain See Together

When we think of seeing, we usually think of our eyes. We imagine they work like the cameras on our smartphones—capturing a perfect, objective photograph of the world in front of us.

In reality, vision is far more wonderful and complex.

Your eye and its retina act as the raw sensor, catching particles of light called photons. This physical recording is sent down the optic nerve as electrical signals. But those signals are just raw, unedited data. The actual "picture" you see is painted by your brain, specifically in a region called the visual cortex.

Because of this amazing division of labor, vision is not a passive recording; it is an active construction. Your brain takes the raw light from your eyes and blends it with your:

- Memory and past experiences: Helping you instantly recognize a flower or a face.
- Emotions and mood: Determining which details you focus on and which colors feel "bright" or "dull."
- Expectations: Predicting what should be there, sometimes filling in gaps before your eyes even register them.

This is why two people with perfectly healthy, identical eyes can stand in front of the exact same painting at the Tallinn exhibition and describe their visual experiences differently. Our brains are individual artists, constantly painting our own unique versions of reality.

## Vision as Emotion and Movement

Vincent van Gogh understood this deeply, even if he didn't know the modern neuroscience behind it. He did not paint objects as they statically existed in the physical world. Instead, he painted how those objects made him feel.

If a cypress tree felt like a green flame reaching for the heavens, that is how he drew it. If a night sky felt like an emotional, turbulent sea, he painted it in thick, swirling brushstrokes. In Van Gogh's world, color ceased to be just a wavelength of light—it became a direct language of the human soul.

When you stand inside the VR exhibition, the moving digital art mimics this emotional movement. It forces us to ask a beautiful question: What if every single one of us is already living inside our own unique, constructed visual reality?
